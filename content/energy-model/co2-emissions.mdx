Carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases released by energy use are the main driver of the warming of
the earth's atmosphere and oceans. Reducing those emissions means reducing them across buildings,
industry and transportation. One useful benchmark suggests that by 2030 each person will need to keep to an
annual "carbon budget" of roughly 2.3 tons-CO<sub>2e</sub> for all activities: about 1 ton for housing and
building use, 1 ton for transportation and 0.3 tons for food. For scale, a single US-to-Europe round-trip
flight releases about 4 tons. With an average occupancy of approximately
<Var k="building.total_num_occupants" /> people, this house would ideally emit less than about
<Var k="narrative.co2.target_tons" /> tons-CO<sub>2e</sub> per year from its operation.

The totals below are estimated from the modeled energy use for heating, cooling, hot water and all
other electric loads. Every variant is all-electric, so all of the emissions come from the electricity
supplied to the house.

The electricity emission factor used for every variant is
**<Var k="narrative.user_defined.co2_factor_kg_kwh" /> kg-CO<sub>2e</sub>/kWh**
(<Var k="narrative.user_defined.co2_factor_lb_mwh" /> lb/MWh), the total output emission rate for the
<strong><Var k="narrative.co2.epa_subgrid_name" /></strong> eGRID subregion, which serves western Pennsylvania, from
<Var k="narrative.user_defined.egrid_edition" />. We use the total output rate, as EPA recommends for estimating the
emissions from a building's electricity use, and the CO<sub>2e</sub> rate, which includes methane and nitrous oxide
as well as CO<sub>2</sub>. No allowance is added for transmission and distribution losses. Source energy factors are
from the EPA ENERGY STAR Portfolio Manager Technical Reference.

For this house the code-minimum variant emits about 15.8 tons-CO<sub>2e</sub> per year, the As-Drawn house about
12.2 tons and the recommended Phius 2024 package about 8.1 tons, roughly half of code minimum. That is still well above
the <Var k="narrative.co2.target_tons" />-ton benchmark. No envelope package closes the remaining gap on this grid.
On-site solar PV is the practical way to do it: offsetting about 5 tons a year takes roughly 12,000 kWh/yr of
generation, or an array of about 9 to 10 kW in Pittsburgh. That is worth carrying into the PV feasibility study the
architect has already identified.

RFC West still generates a large share of its electricity from coal and natural gas. Its emission rate is about
1.5 times that of eastern Pennsylvania (RFCE, 599 lb/MWh), so each kWh saved here avoids more carbon than it would
in Philadelphia, New York or New England. The grid is getting cleaner over time,
and because this house is all-electric its operating emissions will fall with it. On-site solar PV, which
the architect has identified for a feasibility study, would reduce them further.
