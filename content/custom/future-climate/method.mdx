### The hourly weather file

1. **Base year.** The [Climate.OneBuilding TMYx](https://climate.onebuilding.org/WMO_Region_4_North_and_Central_America/USA_United_States_of_America/PA_Pennsylvania/USA_PA_Pittsburgh.Intl.AP.725200_TMYx.2011-2025.zip)
   typical year for Pittsburgh International Airport (WMO 725200), assembled from observed weather between 2011 and 2025.
2. **Monthly change.** The ORNL future weather files for Allegheny County are averaged by month for 2040 to 2059 and
   for 2000 to 2019, for both air temperature and dew point, and subtracted. That gives twelve monthly changes for each.
3. **Smooth seasonal curve.** A smooth curve is fitted through the twelve monthly changes and applied to every hour of
   the year. Applying the changes as flat monthly steps would make the weather jump at every month boundary.
4. **Shift.** Every hour's temperature and dew point is raised by the curve; relative humidity and sky radiation are
   recalculated to match. Sun, cloud, wind and rain are left unchanged.

This "morphing" method is established in the research literature and is the method CIBSE uses for the UK's 2025 future
weather files. It keeps today's observed sunshine, which matters here: the solar data inside the ORNL files is copied
from a 1973 to 2005 record and runs about 13% below what Pittsburgh receives today.

**Solar radiation is not projected.** No published solar projection exists for Pittsburgh. Both future climates use
today's observed solar radiation.

### The PHPP climate

The Passive House energy model works from monthly values, so it gets the monthly averages of the same curve, applied
to the Phius climate data set. One correction comes first. The Phius data for Pittsburgh matches the weather of the
1980s and 1990s: about 2.6°F colder and noticeably drier than observed 2011 to 2025 weather. Adding only the
mid-century change to it would land close to today's climate, not 2050's. So the future PHPP climate is built in two
steps:

1. **Vintage correction:** bring the Phius monthly temperature and dew point up to today's observed climate.
2. **Climate change:** add the mid-century monthly change.

The result averages <Var k="narrative.user_defined.future_annual_mean_F" />°F
(<Var k="narrative.user_defined.future_annual_mean_C" />°C) for the year, against
<Var k="narrative.user_defined.phius_annual_mean_F" />°F for the standard Phius data, and it matches the hourly future
weather file, so the energy and resilience assessments share one future climate.

it combines the warming that has already happened (about 2.6°F) and the mid-century change (about 2.8°F).
