The primary role of airtightness is to keep warm, moist indoor air out of the walls and roof in winter, where it can
condense and cause mold and moisture damage. In Pittsburgh's humid summers it works the other way, keeping humid
outdoor air out of the house. That cuts the energy needed for cooling and dehumidification and improves comfort and
resilience.

Airtightness also has a simple, linear relationship to heat loss: the tighter the construction, the less heat is lost in
winter and the better the energy performance.

The <VarLink hrefKey="narrative.energy_code.code_airtightness_url" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"><Var k="narrative.energy_code.name" /></VarLink>
sets a blower-door limit of <Var k="narrative.energy_code.ach_limit" /> ACH50 (air changes per hour at 50 Pa) for new
houses in <Var k="narrative.energy_code.zone" />, subject to the Pennsylvania amendments in force at permit. The architect's narrative sets a
stricter goal of <Var k="narrative.user_defined.architect_ach_target" /> ACH50, preferably 1.0, with staged
blower-door testing.

We recommend the project target **<Var k="narrative.certification.ph_ach_limit" /> ACH50 or better**. Improving
airtightness to this level is one of the most cost-effective steps available, and it increases comfort and durability
while reducing the risk of uncontrolled wintertime air leakage. On this house, 1.0 ACH50 is also very close to the
Phius limit of <Var k="narrative.certification.phius_cfm50_limit" /> CFM50 per ft² of enclosure, so the recommended
target meets both measures.

<div class="btwr-callout">
  <strong>Design implication</strong>
  <span>
    The As-Drawn assemblies already place a continuous air barrier at the plywood sheathing, outboard of the framing
    and the services. That is the right location. The risk on this house is at the transitions: the many roof
    planes and dormers, the bay window, the balcony over conditioned space, the elevator overrun and the junction
    with the garage. Each needs an air-barrier detail before construction documents, and each should be inspected
    at the first (pre-drywall) blower-door test.
  </span>
</div>

Write the 1.0 ACH50 target into the construction documents as a contractual requirement, with at least two tests:
one once the air barrier is complete and still accessible, and a final test at completion. The first test is the one
that matters. Leaks found at that stage are cheap to fix.
