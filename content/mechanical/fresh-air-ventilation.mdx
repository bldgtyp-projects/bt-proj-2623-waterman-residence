title: "Fresh-Air Ventilation"
dek: "Describe the balanced ventilation strategy and the reasoning behind it."

An airtight, low-energy house in this climate needs dedicated mechanical ventilation with heat recovery to deliver
enough fresh air for good indoor air quality in every season. The architect's narrative already includes this: a
Zehnder ComfoAir Q energy-recovery ventilator (ERV), running continuously, on its own ductwork.

In Pittsburgh an ERV, which recovers moisture as well as heat, is the right choice over a heat-recovery ventilator
(HRV). In winter it keeps indoor air from becoming too dry. In summer it keeps much of the outdoor humidity out, which
reduces the dehumidification load on the heat pumps. The step from HRV to ERV is the
[Improved Mech.](/energy_model/#model-variants) variant in this report.

It is important not to over-ventilate a house in winter, or the indoor air becomes too dry. To keep airflow rates low
while still maintaining good indoor air quality, we recommend a **Supply-Transfer-Extract** configuration rather than
supplying and extracting from every room.

Supply air goes to bedrooms, living spaces and offices; extract air is drawn from bathrooms, laundry rooms and the
kitchen. Transfer openings, such as door undercuts or transfer grilles, connect supply and extract spaces.

This configuration reduces duct-borne sound between rooms, ensures good mixing of fresh air, and reduces the size,
complexity and cost of the ductwork.

<div class="btwr-callout btwr-callout--critical">
  <strong>Coordination note</strong>
  <span>
    The fresh-air ventilation system must be installed completely independently of the heating and cooling system.
    There is no effective way to combine the fresh-air ducting with the heating and cooling ducting, so the design
    should allocate space for both. On a four-level house with an elevator, plan the vertical duct chases early.
  </span>
</div>
