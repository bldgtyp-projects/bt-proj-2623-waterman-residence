In summer the risk shifts to the west and to the upper floors:

- **The upper west-side windows receive the most summer radiation on the house**, from low afternoon sun above the
  neighboring roof. These are the hottest windows in the study.
- **The sunroom doors stay high in summer**, as they were in winter. With the sunroom's large glass area, it is the
  room most likely to overheat.
- **The rear stair-tower and dormer windows drop to a moderate level.** The high summer sun is less direct on them.
- **The rear bay window is well protected.** The balcony above it blocks the high summer sun while it still receives
  winter sun. This is exactly the behavior to aim for elsewhere.
- **The street facade stays low** all year.

The third-floor rooms, including the studio and the snug under the cathedral ceilings, sit above the neighbors'
shade. Combined with the warm air that rises to the top of a three-story house, they are the rooms most likely to run
warm in summer and early fall.

- Prioritize shading on the third-floor and dormer windows, and on the south- and west-facing glass of the upper floors.
- Exterior shading works roughly three times as well as interior shading, because it stops the sun before it enters
  the house. Where exterior shades are not practical, use robust, user-operable interior blinds with high opacity
  and solar reflectance.
- Specify glazing with a low SHGC (g-value) on unshaded west-facing and upper-floor glass.
- Pittsburgh's summer nights are warm and humid, so opening windows at night helps less than it does in a dry
  climate. Shading, plus a heat pump sized for dehumidification, is the dependable strategy.
