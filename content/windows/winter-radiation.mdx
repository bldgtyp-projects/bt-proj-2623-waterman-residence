The study shows where the useful winter gain lands:

- **The rear facade collects most of it, and mostly on the upper floors.** The tall stair-tower windows and the
  third-floor dormer on the rear, which sit above the neighboring roofs, receive the most winter sun on the house. The
  first-floor bay window collects a moderate amount. Keep all of this glass unshaded in winter.
- **The sunroom doors receive strong sun in both seasons.** That helps in winter and is a summer risk; see the next
  section.
- **The street facade receives almost none.** Its windows face north-northeast and show the lowest radiation on the
  house all winter. They lose heat all season without any useful gain in return. A 5% to 10% reduction in north glazing
  area, where it is not needed for daylight or the facade composition, would benefit the overall energy balance.
- **The side walls are mostly shaded.** Within a few feet of the neighbors, the lower side windows receive very little
  sun. Only the upper side windows, above the neighboring roofs, see meaningful radiation.

Pittsburgh's winters are cloudy, so winter solar gain is modest even on the best glass. Window U-value matters more
here than solar gain for most of the house.
