The energy balance of the windows is critical on any high-performance building. In Pittsburgh's climate the goal is
to collect some useful winter sun to reduce heating, while staying cautious about summer overheating and the extra
cooling and dehumidification it brings. Rooms with large amounts of south- or west-facing glass need the most
attention.

All radiation values presented consider the local shading context. The surrounding houses are modeled as simple
massing blocks built from satellite images from Google Maps and building footprints from
[OpenStreetMap](https://www.openstreetmap.org/) and [CadMapper](https://cadmapper.com/). The two large trees in the
rear yards are modeled as solid shapes, and the site survey sets the ground and lot lines.

The shading context for this site:

1. **Close neighbors on both sides.** The side yards are only about 4 to 5 ft wide. The three-story house at 6914
   McPherson and the two-story house at 6922 shade the side-facing windows on the lower floors for most of the day,
   and almost entirely in winter, when the sun is low.
2. **Orientation.** The street facade faces north-northeast and the rear garden faces south-southwest. The rear
   elevation is the main opportunity for winter solar gain, and also the main source of summer and afternoon heat.
3. **Rear-yard trees.** Two large trees south of the house shade the lower rear windows. They are modeled as solid,
   which is conservative in winter, when deciduous trees let most of the sun through.
4. **Porches, balcony and overhangs.** The front porch, screened porch and second-floor balcony shade the glass
   beneath them year-round: useful in summer, a cost in winter.

The upper floors see much less shading from the neighbors than the lower floors. As the radiation studies in the
next two sections show, they get the most winter sun and the most summer heat.
